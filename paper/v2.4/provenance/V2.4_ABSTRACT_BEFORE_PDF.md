### Abstract

How can a pattern or learned response be rebuilt after damage or replacement? Using computer models, we distinguish what past experience leaves behind, what a system does now, and what a replacement can recover. We call the information carried forward a record, and test how systems use it and write a new one.

In spatial models, records of patterns formed without a supplied target image help replacements recover features specific to the original pattern. This modest effect persists through sixteen replacements, even as reconstruction becomes less accurate. Surviving body activity contributes message-specific information within supplied capture and reset procedures. A fixed writing window improves both the body and a fresh recipient after complete record corruption, while writing useful records can cause harm.

In a network model, a fixed repair procedure raises success in one fresh reconstruction from 81.52% to 87.45%. Across many futures from the same starting records, repeated repair raises terminal reconstruction after eighteen further rebuilds from 87.52% to 91.72%, although some cases are harmed. Tests of a record's immediate successor improve forecasts of later reconstruction beyond controls using the same number of reading trials. Conversely, an intervention that improves the current response can impair its successor. Communicating cells sharing a coarse regulatory classification can also differ in their ability to pass it to replacements.

Retaining information, expressing it now and using it to rebuild later are different capacities. Reconstruction depends on the receiving machinery and on when current activity rewrites memory. Exploratory multiscale tests show both sides of this feedback: a body can maintain its record, but can also teach it distortions. These controlled models identify useful interventions and consequential failures; a general predictive theory, autonomous maintenance and validation in living systems remain open.
