Fresh-assay reliability remains uncertain even with packet bytes fixed. In the simultaneous 48-interval family, the two late packets with 47/64 and 51/64 successes have ancestral-success intervals [0.5259, 0.8880] and [0.5956, 0.9285]. A packet with 64/64 successes has lower bound 0.8886; a packet with 0/64 has upper bound 0.1114. These bounds condition on the particular packets and nuisance protocol. They are not population estimates or confidence bounds for later full rewriting. The two negative between-packet variance estimates were retained as sampling artifacts; neither is interpreted as negative physical variance.

The observation counterexample clarifies what the measurements identify. One packet process always produces a packet whose fresh assays pass independently with probability .75. Another produces a deterministically readable or unreadable packet at each renewal, with probabilities .75/.25. From a common initially robust source, their single-assay histories and later marginal probabilities coincide, and the existing two-mode HMM represents that shared law. Duplicate assays after renewal disagree with probability .375 in the first process and zero in the second. Thus repeatability data help distinguish persistent packet state from fresh-body variability; variable assays alone do not refute the HMM.

The strongest finite-information warning occurs even inside a simple candidate submodel. With irreversible readiness loss, no recovery and 16 independent two-renewal paths, observing no short loss gives a two-sided exact 95% interval of **[0.025, 1]** for horizon-32 success. In the perfectly readable symmetric-flip submodel, the corresponding interval is [0.500193, 1]. Neither supports a precise near-perfect forecast. In the absorbing model, even the best case requires 561 all-successful short paths for that lower bound to exceed .9. This is conditional arithmetic, not a native sample-size recommendation or an instruction to collect until a desired answer appears.

Abstention requires its own calibration. Across the declared panel, ordinary exact intervals had minimum unconditional coverage 95.0802%; Wilson intervals reached 85.1802%. Selecting only exact intervals at most .10 wide could make coverage among issued intervals zero. At absorbing-loss hazard .05, n=1024 and H=32, unconditional coverage was 95.4611%, but the rule issued only 2.1241% of the time and all those issued intervals missed the true value. The overall error probability stayed below 5%, so this is compatible with the unconditional guarantee.

Conditioning the tail inversion on that original selection event repaired the selected-case calculation. At the same setting, conditional coverage became 97.7780%, mean corrected width was 0.3246, and 99.4770% of corrected intervals were wider than the original .10 target. No second width filter was applied. The minimum corrected conditional coverage across the complete panel was 95.2003%. This establishes a useful distinction: a valid correction can require admitting that the promised precision was unavailable. The stated guarantee remains conditional on the restricted binomial model and the fixed selection rule.
